## The challenge

Designit is a global strategic design consultancy that helps organizations through complex change. In late summer 2022, the Strategic Experience Design team in North America wanted new ways to attract clients and move into domains of long-term interest. The Futures team set out to make its thinking public with a point of view.

<p class="hmw">How do you make speculative futures legible and credible when there's no precedent?</p>

## The process as a prototype

We designed a series of exercises to guide the work, and treated the process itself as a prototype. With three weeks, this first version favored learning by making, experimenting, and trusting the method.

### Grounding

Understanding the past to avoid repeating it. We studied the history of agriculture and our relationship to food to find the long-term patterns, cultural shifts, and structural forces that still shape how food is grown and eaten.

### Sensing

Separating what persists from what can change. We sorted what we found into constants, variables, and trends, which became the building blocks for every future story.

<div class="cols3">
  <div><h4>Constants</h4><p>Needs that won't change in 100 years: water, food, belonging.</p></div>
  <div><h4>Variables</h4><p>Conditions that shift: crop yields, technology, where farming happens.</p></div>
  <div><h4>Trends</h4><p>What's emerging: smart-crop monitoring, drones, the push for sustainability.</p></div>
</div>

### Drivers of change

Mapping pressures across scales. To get past surface-level trends, we used ARUP's 150 Drivers of Change, a set of social, technological, economic, environmental, and political forces. We mapped them at local, national, and global scales, then connected them into narratives specific to agriculture.

### Narratives

From connected drivers to plausible futures. By tracing paths between drivers, we wrote nine future narratives about how water scarcity, climate stress, urbanization, and energy might interact if left unchecked. Three shared a pattern, so we combined them into one larger arc: rapid industrialization, geopolitical conflict, and extreme climate change, with water connecting global forces to local impact.

### Reframing

Shifting from inevitability to agency. We turned the bleakest outcomes into How Might We questions, which moved us from watching possible futures to finding where design, policy, and technology could step in.

<figure class="wide"><img src="/work/futures-assets/reframing.webp" alt="Four How Might We questions: design water-centric communities; use technology to accommodate vertical farming; stabilize water supplies and manage virtual water; and depend on our human need for joy and connection"/></figure>

## Three futures set in 2122

We turned the systems into illustrated vignettes set 100 years out. They were meant to provoke conversation, challenge assumptions, and get organizations thinking about how today's decisions shape the long term. Each one explores a different relationship to water, technology, and land.

## Beyond the concept

Grounded in agriculture, the point of view still translated across domains. In three weeks, it:

- Opened conversations with new client domains: CPG, pharma, and the public sector.
- Fed discussions with Los Angeles World Airports about a new employee experience, app, and brand ahead of the FIFA World Cup and the Olympic Games.
- Positioned Designit as a strategic partner for long-term challenges.
- Became an internal tool for designers to share future-focused thinking.

## Reflection

**Futures work reveals leverage.** Designing for the future isn't only about imagining outcomes. Grounded in systems thinking, uncertainty became a place to act on purpose.

**A shared language.** The point of view gave the team a way to talk about long-term challenges and turn abstract change into a strategic choice.
